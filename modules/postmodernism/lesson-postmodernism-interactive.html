The World That Made Postmodernism

When Modernism Became the Establishment

By the late 1960s, modernism had won so completely that it had ceased to be rebellious. The glass-and-steel corporate tower, the open-plan office, the minimalist interior, and the Helvetica-on-a-grid annual report had become the dominant visual language of Western capitalism. A young designer in 1970 looking at a Mies tower saw a building that insurance companies liked. The revolution had become the mortgage.

Two things happened in response. First, a wave of rigorous criticism from inside the profession itself — above all Robert Venturi's 1966 book Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture and his 1972 sequel Learning from Las Vegas, co-written with Denise Scott Brown. Second, a wave of playful, sometimes outrageous object-making from Milan — Studio Alchymia, and then in 1981 the Memphis Group. These two currents, plus a parallel rebellion in graphic design spreading out of Basel and Cranbrook, combined to form what we now call postmodernism.

Less Is a Bore
Venturi's reply to Mies van der Rohe's "Less is more." A joke that became a manifesto: modernism's purity had become an aesthetic trap, and design needed to reclaim ornament, color, and historical reference.
Learning from Las Vegas
Venturi, Scott Brown & Izenour's 1972 book argued that the Las Vegas Strip was legitimate vernacular architecture. The book's distinction between the "duck" and the "decorated shed" became one of the most productive ideas in twentieth-century design theory.
The Memphis Moment
In September 1981 Milan, Ettore Sottsass and a group of younger designers exhibited brightly laminated furniture that looked like toys. Within a year, Memphis had colonized fashion, graphic design, and early MTV.
The Macintosh Arrives
1984. Suddenly typographic experimentation was possible without typesetters or photomechanical transfer. April Greiman and Emigre pushed the new tools to their limit, producing layered, layered-on-layered compositions that would have been impossible — or prohibitively expensive — a decade earlier.

Defining Characteristics

Historical quotation: columns, pediments, classical motifs plucked out of context and reassembled with ironic distance. Ornament welcomed back: pattern and decoration returned as legitimate tools after seventy years of modernist prohibition. Clashing color: pastels, electric turquoise, brick red and mustard together — the colors of motel roadsides and 1950s diners reclaimed as avant-garde material. Contradiction as method: a single object might mix incompatible styles, materials, or voices on purpose. Irony and wit: the willingness to be funny in serious professional contexts, which modernism had mostly forbidden. Honesty abandoned: plastic laminate printed to look like marble, sheet metal pretending to be stone, plywood pretending to be mahogany — "honesty to materials" recast as an ideology rather than a law.

c. 1995Postmodernism absorbed into stock commercial style; new minimalism and web design take over
Think About It

Postmodernism argued that modernism had become the establishment. Today, many of postmodernism's own gestures — irony, historical quotation, playful color — are themselves the establishment of brand culture and consumer design. What would a rebellion against contemporary design orthodoxy look like? What are the untouchable rules of design in 2026 that nobody is allowed to break?

Key Figures

The People of Postmodernism

Robert Venturi & Denise Scott Brown
1925–2018 & b. 1931 · Philadelphia

Architects & Theorists

The intellectual founders of architectural postmodernism. Venturi's Complexity and Contradiction (1966) and their co-authored Learning from Las Vegas (1972) reframed architecture as a communicative act. Scott Brown's contribution was long under-credited due to the sexism of the profession.

Michael Graves
1934–2015 · Princeton, NJ
Click to flip
Architect & Product Designer

The postmodern architect whose work most successfully colonized popular culture. Broke from his early rigorous modernism in the late 1970s. His Portland Building (1982) was the first monumental built postmodern commission. Later designed thousands of household objects for Alessi and Target.

Ettore Sottsass
1917–2007 · Milan
Click to flip
Designer & Theorist

Founder of the Memphis Group (1981). Spent two decades as a serious modernist consultant to Olivetti before turning his attention to questions modernism had ignored: emotion, play, vulgarity, and the cultural weight of cheap materials. His laminate totems are now in museum collections worldwide.

Alessandro Mendini
1931–2019 · Milan
Click to flip
Designer & Editor

Founded Studio Alchymia (1976), predecessor to Memphis. Edited Casabella, Modo, and Domus. His Proust armchair — a Baroque chair overpainted in Pointillist dots — is one of the clearest demonstrations of postmodern methodology.

Philip Johnson
1906–2005 · New York
Click to flip
Architect & Stylistic Impresario

Switched from modernism to postmodernism to deconstructivism over his long career, always positioning himself near whichever movement was winning. His AT&T Building (1984) put postmodernism on the cover of Time magazine and made corporate clients take it seriously.

James Stirling
1926–1992 · United Kingdom
Click to flip
Architect

The most important European architect to turn postmodern. His Neue Staatsgalerie in Stuttgart (1984) is widely regarded as the finest postmodern building anywhere — its rigorous composition holds together where weaker postmodernism would collapse into pastiche.

Charles Moore
1925–1993 · USA
Click to flip
Architect & Teacher

American architect whose Piazza d'Italia in New Orleans (1978) was one of postmodernism's most joyous public projects. A long teaching career at UCLA and Yale shaped a generation of architects to take pleasure and vernacular reference seriously.

Wolfgang Weingart
1941–2021 · Basel
Click to flip
Typographer & Teacher

The Swiss typographer who broke the Swiss grid from within. Taught at the Basel School of Design from 1968, systematically violating the rules of the International Style he had been trained in. His students — above all April Greiman — carried the experiments to America.

April Greiman
b. 1948 · Los Angeles
Click to flip
Graphic Designer

Trained with Weingart in Basel, moved to Los Angeles in 1976. Fused Weingart's typographic experiments with a Californian embrace of color, video imagery, and — crucially — the early Macintosh. Her 1986 issue of Design Quarterly was a manifesto object for computer-native graphic design.

Katherine McCoy
b. 1945 · Cranbrook, MI
Click to flip
Designer & Educator

Co-chair of Cranbrook Academy's graphic design program (1971–1995). Presided over the most theoretically ambitious graphic design education of the era: students read Derrida and Barthes alongside type specimens. Cranbrook's output defined "deconstructed" graphic design in America.

Neville Brody
b. 1957 · London
Click to flip
Graphic Designer & Type Designer

Art director of British style magazine The Face (1981–1986). Designed every issue as a typographic experiment and drew custom typefaces for each project. The most influential magazine design of the decade, and the point at which postmodern typography crossed into mainstream youth culture.

Peter Eisenman
b. 1932 · New York / New Haven
Click to flip
Architect & Theorist

The bridge figure from postmodernism to deconstructivism. His Wexner Center for the Arts (1989) — two grids colliding at an angle, walls cut away — marks the point where postmodernism's interest in quotation gives way to deconstructivism's interest in formal rupture.

Ideas & Principles

What Made It "Postmodern"

Irony, ornament, quotation, contradiction

Postmodernism was never a single manifesto. It was a coordinated rebellion across architecture, furniture, product design, graphic design, and theory, animated by a shared exhaustion with modernist orthodoxy. These principles travelled across disciplines and gave the movement its family resemblance.

Historical Quotation
Columns, pediments, patterns
Click to flip
Method

Postmodern designers raided the history of architecture and decorative art the way a DJ samples old records. A column might be Doric, Tuscan, or cartoon-stylized. A pattern might reference Art Deco or a 1950s Formica counter. The quotation was rarely straightforward homage; it was almost always at one ironic remove.

Ornament Welcomed Back
"Ornament is no longer a crime"
Click to flip
Philosophy

Adolf Loos had declared ornament a crime in 1908. Modernism took him seriously for seventy years. Postmodernism reversed the verdict. Applied pattern, molding, decoration, and explicit color returned as legitimate tools — not as revived craft ornament, but as deliberate, often cheap-looking quotation.

The Decorated Shed
Venturi, Scott Brown & Izenour, 1972
Click to flip
Concept

A "duck" is a building whose form is its own sign (like the Long Island duck-shaped restaurant). A "decorated shed" is a conventional structure that communicates through applied signage. Learning from Las Vegas argued that most real architecture is and should be decorated sheds, and that modernism's obsession with duck-like pure form had been an expensive mistake.

Clashing Color
Pastels, neon, "vulgar" hues
Click to flip
Palette

Postmodernism embraced exactly the colors modernism had ruled out: pastel pinks and mint greens, electric turquoise, brick red and mustard together, salmon and teal. The colors of motel roadsides and 1950s diners were reclaimed as avant-garde material. The vulgarity was the point.

Contradiction as Method
Incompatible elements on purpose
Click to flip
Strategy

A postmodern building might have a classical facade on one side and a glass curtain wall on another. A postmodern layout might use three typefaces where modernism insisted on one. The simultaneity of incompatible elements was a deliberate refusal of modernism's demand that a good design express a single clear idea.

Irony & Wit
Design allowed to be funny
Click to flip
Tone

Modernism had been serious. Postmodernism wanted to be funny. A kettle with a bird on the spout. A skyscraper with a Chippendale pediment. A bookshelf shaped like a prehistoric totem. The willingness to make visual jokes in serious professional contexts was itself a departure.

Honesty Abandoned
Laminate pretending to be marble
Click to flip
Material Strategy

Modernism insisted that materials should be expressed honestly. Postmodernism dropped the rule. Memphis pieces were particleboard wrapped in laminate printed to look like marble, terrazzo, or cartoon patterns. The insistence on material honesty had itself been an ideology, not a law of nature.

Deconstructed Type
The Swiss grid broken from within
Click to flip
Graphic Method

Weingart set type at angles and at exaggerated letter-spacing; Greiman layered bitmap imagery on a Mac; Brody drew custom typefaces for every issue; Cranbrook students layered text over text so readers constructed meaning rather than receiving it. The discipline of the Swiss grid was replaced by the discipline of the well-argued contradiction.

Think About It

Postmodernism's favorite tool was irony — making a gesture while simultaneously signaling that you didn't quite mean it. Irony depends on the audience sharing your distance from the thing being quoted. When does that distance collapse? What happens to an ironic gesture when people start to take it seriously, or when people forget what the original joke was about?
