Art Nouveau: The New Art

c. 1890–1910
DSGN 230 — Survey of Design History

Introduction — A Style That Wanted Everything

Art Nouveau was greedy. It wanted to reshape everything — not just paintings on a wall or ornament on a building, but the building itself, and the furniture inside it, and the wallpaper behind the furniture, and the lamp on the table, and the clasp on the woman’s dress, and the poster advertising the play she was going to see. It wanted, in a word borrowed from German opera, Gesamtkunstwerk — the total work of art.

Emerging in the early 1890s and fading by around 1910, Art Nouveau was the first truly international modern design style. It appeared almost simultaneously across Europe and the United States under a constellation of regional names — Art Nouveau in France and Belgium, Jugendstil in Germany, Sezessionstil in Austria, Stile Liberty in Italy, Modernisme in Catalonia, the Glasgow Style in Scotland, Nieuwe Kunst in the Netherlands. Despite these local flavors, the movement shared a core conviction: that the nineteenth century’s habit of copying historical styles was exhausted, and that a genuinely new art — an art nouveau — was both possible and necessary.

What made it new was where it looked for inspiration. Not to Greek columns or Gothic arches, but to nature — to the curve of a vine, the veining of a leaf, the spiral of a seashell, the iridescence of a dragonfly wing. Art Nouveau’s great formal innovation was the whiplash line: a sinuous, asymmetric, organic curve that seemed to pulse with life. That line ran through everything the movement touched.

Part 1 — Historical Context: The World That Made Art Nouveau

The Fin de Siècle

Art Nouveau emerged during the Belle Époque, the period of relative peace and prosperity in Western Europe between roughly 1871 and 1914. But beneath the surface optimism ran currents of anxiety. The final decade of the nineteenth century — the fin de siècle — was an era preoccupied with endings and beginnings. Cities were expanding at disorienting speed. New technologies — electric light, the telephone, the automobile, cinema — were transforming daily experience. There was a widespread sense that an old world was dissolving and something unknown was taking its place.

This atmosphere produced two competing impulses in art and design. One was a retreat toward the handmade and the medieval, embodied by the Arts and Crafts movement. The other was an embrace of modernity — but a modernity that refused to be ugly. Art Nouveau took the second path. It accepted new materials and industrial techniques but insisted they could produce beauty, not by imitating the past, but by inventing new forms.

The Shadow of Arts and Crafts

Art Nouveau owed a significant debt to the Arts and Crafts movement that preceded it. William Morris and his circle had established two ideas that Art Nouveau inherited directly: first, that the decorative arts deserved the same respect as fine art, and second, that the designer should aim for a unified aesthetic environment rather than isolated objects. Where Art Nouveau broke from Arts and Crafts was in its relationship to the machine. Morris had rejected industrial production. Art Nouveau practitioners, for the most part, embraced it — or at least accepted it as a reality to be shaped rather than resisted.

Japonisme: The Eastern Catalyst

One of the most powerful influences on Art Nouveau came from an unexpected direction. Following Japan’s forced reopening to Western trade in the 1850s, Japanese art — particularly ukiyo-e woodblock prints — flooded into Europe. By the 1880s and 1890s, the enthusiasm for Japanese aesthetics, known as Japonisme, had permeated painting, printmaking, and the decorative arts.

What European artists found in Japanese prints was revelatory: flat areas of bold color, asymmetric compositions, the use of empty space as a compositional element, natural motifs treated with stylized elegance rather than botanical literalism, and a refusal to separate the “useful” from the “beautiful.” These principles mapped directly onto Art Nouveau’s emerging sensibility. The dragonflies, butterflies, irises, and chrysanthemums that populate Art Nouveau design owe as much to Hiroshige and Hokusai as to any European botanical tradition.

The 1900 Paris Exposition Universelle

If any single event marked Art Nouveau’s peak, it was the 1900 Exposition Universelle in Paris. Attracting over 50 million visitors, the exposition was a vast showcase of industrial progress, cultural ambition, and — everywhere — Art Nouveau. The style permeated pavilions, interiors, decorative objects, and graphic materials. Hector Guimard’s sinuous cast-iron Métro entrances, installed around the same time, became permanent symbols of the movement’s ambition to merge art with everyday infrastructure.

But the exposition also foreshadowed Art Nouveau’s limitations. Critics began to complain that the style had become excessive, even decadent. The very ubiquity that demonstrated its success also planted the seeds of its decline.

Siegfried Bing and the Name Itself

The term “Art Nouveau” traces directly to the German-born, Paris-based art dealer Siegfried Bing, who in 1895 renamed his gallery Maison de l’Art Nouveau. Bing championed both Japanese art and the emerging European modernists, and his gallery became a crucial meeting point between the two. Henry van de Velde designed interiors for the space. Émile Gallé, Louis Comfort Tiffany, and others exhibited there. The gallery’s name stuck to the movement itself — at least in France and Belgium.

Part 2 — Defining Characteristics: How to Recognize Art Nouveau

The Whiplash Line

Art Nouveau’s most immediately recognizable feature is its line: sinuous, asymmetric, organic, and dynamic. Often described as the “whiplash” or “coup de fouet” curve, this undulating S-form appears in ironwork, furniture, illustration, jewelry, and architecture. It was not purely decorative — it expressed the movement’s philosophical commitment to nature as the ultimate design source.

Natural Motifs

Flora and fauna dominate Art Nouveau’s visual vocabulary. Flowers (lilies, irises, orchids, poppies), vines and tendrils, insects (dragonflies, butterflies, beetles), birds (peacocks, swans), flowing water, and the female form rendered with botanical fluidity — all appear constantly. These motifs were not simply applied as surface decoration. In the best Art Nouveau work, structure and ornament are inseparable: an iron railing becomes a vine, a lamp becomes a flower, a building facade becomes a living surface.

Gesamtkunstwerk — The Total Work of Art

The German concept of Gesamtkunstwerk, originally associated with Richard Wagner’s vision of opera as a synthesis of all art forms, became central to Art Nouveau practice. Architects did not merely design buildings; they designed everything inside them — furniture, lighting, textiles, hardware, even cutlery. Victor Horta’s townhouses in Brussels are the clearest expression of this ideal: from the iron columns to the mosaic floors to the stained-glass skylights to the door handles, every element participates in a single organic vision.

Materials Used Expressively

Art Nouveau practitioners pushed materials beyond their conventional limits. Wrought iron was bent into organic tendrils. Glass was shaped, layered, and chemically treated to achieve effects never seen before — Émile Gallé’s cameo glass, Tiffany’s opalescent Favrile glass, the iridescent Bohemian glass of Loetz. Ceramic tile flowed across building facades. Wood was carved into sinuous furniture forms. Enamel, semi-precious stones, and horn appeared in jewelry that blurred the line between fine art and personal adornment.

Part 3 — People: The Key Figures

Alphonse Mucha (1860–1939) — Prague and Paris

Mucha, a Czech-born artist working in Paris, became Art Nouveau’s most famous graphic voice almost overnight. On December 26, 1894, he was called upon to produce an emergency poster for Sarah Bernhardt’s production of Gismonda. The resulting work — a tall, narrow lithograph depicting the actress in flowing Byzantine-inspired robes surrounded by ornamental arches and mosaic patterns — was an instant sensation. Bernhardt was so impressed she signed Mucha to an exclusive six-year contract.

Mucha’s subsequent posters for Bernhardt — Médée (1898), La Tosca (1898), Hamlet (1899) — along with his commercial work for products like Job cigarette papers and Moët champagne, defined the Art Nouveau poster: idealized female figures with cascading hair, elaborate decorative borders, flattened perspective, and a palette of muted pastels punctuated by gold. His “Mucha style” became so synonymous with the era that Art Nouveau was sometimes simply called “le style Mucha.”

Victor Horta (1861–1947) — Brussels

If any single building inaugurated Art Nouveau architecture, it was Victor Horta’s Hôtel Tassel in Brussels, completed in 1893. Designed as a private townhouse for the scientist Émile Tassel, the building’s innovation was total: an open floor plan organized around a central staircase, exposed iron structural columns treated as decorative elements, and a continuous vocabulary of organic curves running from the iron balustrades through the mosaic floors to the painted walls and stained-glass ceiling.

Horta went on to design several more Brussels townhouses — the Hôtel Solvay (1894–1898), the Hôtel van Eetvelde (1895–1898), and his own home and studio (now the Horta Museum) — all of which are UNESCO World Heritage Sites. His buildings demonstrated that Art Nouveau was not merely a style of surface decoration but a fundamental rethinking of architectural space.

Hector Guimard (1867–1942) — Paris

Guimard brought Art Nouveau to the Paris streets. His most enduring contribution was the series of cast-iron and glass entrance canopies he designed for the Paris Métro between 1898 and 1901, installed from 1900 to 1913. Produced industrially in three standard designs, these entrances — with their green-painted organic ironwork, amber glass lamps resembling flower buds, and sinuous lettering — became the most visible Art Nouveau landmarks in the world. Parisians initially called the style “le style Guimard,” and later “le style Métro.” Only 86 of the original 167 entrances survive today, now protected as historical monuments.

Antoni Gaudí (1852–1926) — Barcelona

Gaudí stands as arguably the most radical architect Art Nouveau produced — though calling him simply “Art Nouveau” undersells his singularity. Working within the Catalan Modernisme movement, Gaudí pushed architecture into territory that was almost biological. His buildings do not so much stand as grow.

Casa Batlló (1904–1906) reimagined an existing apartment building into something resembling a marine organism — its facade undulates with bone-like balconies and shimmering trencadís (broken-tile mosaic), its roof ridged like the spine of a dragon. Park Güell (begun 1900) transformed a hillside into a landscape of mosaic-encrusted terraces and organic stone colonnades. And the Sagrada Família basilica, which Gaudí took over in 1883 and worked on exclusively from 1914 until his death in 1926 — still under construction a century later — represents perhaps the most ambitious attempt to translate natural forms into monumental architecture ever undertaken.

Louis Comfort Tiffany (1848–1933) — New York

Tiffany translated Art Nouveau into glass with unmatched technical virtuosity. The son of the founder of Tiffany & Co., he developed Favrile glass — a technique using metallic oxides introduced into molten glass to produce iridescent, luminous surfaces that changed color with the light. His stained-glass lamps, commercially produced from about 1895 to 1920, used a copper-foil technique (rather than traditional lead came) that permitted more intricate and organic designs: wisteria cascading down a shade, dragonflies with jeweled wings, poppies and water lilies rendered in opalescent glass.

Tiffany’s work demonstrated that Art Nouveau could flourish within American commercial culture, and his lamps became some of the most recognizable decorative objects of the era.

Émile Gallé (1846–1904) — Nancy, France

Gallé was the leading figure of the École de Nancy, the center of French Art Nouveau outside Paris. A glassmaker, ceramicist, and furniture designer, he developed innovative techniques including glass marquetry — layering colored glass and then etching or engraving through the layers to reveal designs beneath — and pâte de verre, a method of casting finely crushed glass into molds. His vases, lamps, and furniture drew their imagery directly from nature: flowers, insects, landscapes, often with a poetic, almost literary quality. In 1901 he founded and became the first president of the École de Nancy, organizing the region’s Art Nouveau artisans into a formal alliance.

Gustav Klimt (1862–1918) — Vienna

Klimt was the central figure of the Vienna Secession, the Austrian branch of the Art Nouveau movement, which he co-founded in 1897. His paintings fused figural naturalism with flat, ornamental gold surfaces inspired by Byzantine mosaics and Japanese prints. The Kiss (1907–1908), now in Vienna’s Belvedere Palace, is perhaps the single most iconic image associated with the entire Art Nouveau era: two figures wrapped in golden, geometrically patterned robes, dissolving into ornament against a field of flowers. Klimt’s work bridged Art Nouveau’s organic sensibility with the more geometric abstraction that would characterize early modernism.

Charles Rennie Mackintosh (1868–1928) — Glasgow

Mackintosh developed a distinctive variant of Art Nouveau that was cooler, more geometric, and more architectonic than its continental counterparts. His masterwork, the Glasgow School of Art (designed in phases from 1896 to 1909), combined spare rectilinear forms with subtle organic touches — the famous Mackintosh Rose motif, Japanese-influenced spatial arrangements, and a mastery of natural light. His interiors, furniture, and graphic work influenced the Vienna Secessionists significantly and pointed toward the geometric abstraction that would characterize twentieth-century modernism. Mackintosh demonstrated that Art Nouveau did not require extravagant curves to be powerful.

René Lalique (1860–1945) — Paris

Lalique revolutionized jewelry by rejecting the Victorian obsession with precious stones in favor of design-driven pieces using enamel, glass, horn, semi-precious materials, and unconventional imagery. His Art Nouveau jewelry featured dragonflies, orchids, serpents, and the female form rendered with extraordinary technical refinement. He exhibited at Siegfried Bing’s gallery from 1895 and became internationally famous at the 1900 Exposition. Later, beginning around 1907 with commissions for perfumer François Coty, Lalique pivoted to glass production, creating iconic perfume bottles and decorative objects that carried Art Nouveau’s sensibility into the twentieth century.

Aubrey Beardsley (1872–1898) — London

Beardsley’s career lasted barely seven years before his death from tuberculosis at age 25, but his influence was enormous. His black-ink illustrations — for Thomas Malory’s Le Morte d’Arthur (1893), Oscar Wilde’s Salomé (1894), and the literary magazine The Yellow Book (1894) — combined Art Nouveau’s organic line with a darkness, eroticism, and grotesque wit that no other practitioner matched. His style drew from Japanese woodcuts, Greek vase painting, and the Decadent literary movement, producing images that were simultaneously beautiful and disturbing. Beardsley’s work demonstrated that Art Nouveau was not merely decorative — it could be provocative, subversive, and psychologically charged.

Other Essential Figures

Henry van de Velde (1863–1957), a Belgian painter-turned-architect, designed interiors for Bing’s gallery, his own home (Villa Bloemenwerf, 1895–1896), and eventually reorganized the Kunstgewerbeschule in Weimar — the institution that would become the Bauhaus in 1919, creating a direct lineage from Art Nouveau to modernist design education.

Louis Majorelle (1859–1926), based in Nancy alongside Gallé, created furniture inspired by plant stems, water lilies, and tendrils. His work triumphed at the 1900 Exposition and earned him an international reputation. He was a co-founding member of the École de Nancy.

Otto Wagner (1841–1918) designed the stations and infrastructure of the Vienna Stadtbahn (1894–1897), bringing Art Nouveau sensibility to civic architecture. His Postal Savings Bank (1904–1906), with its curving glass roof and aluminum-bolted marble facade, is considered a milestone in the transition from Art Nouveau to modern architecture.

Josef Hoffmann (1870–1956), a student of Wagner and co-founder of the Vienna Secession, established the Wiener Werkstätte (Vienna Workshops) in 1903 with Koloman Moser. The Werkstätte produced furniture, metalwork, ceramics, textiles, and fashion under Art Nouveau and proto-modernist principles, operating until 1932 and forecasting both Art Deco and the International Style.

Part 4 — Places: Regional Variations

Art Nouveau’s international character is one of its most remarkable features. The same impulse — to create a modern style rooted in nature rather than history — produced strikingly different results depending on local traditions, materials, climate, and cultural politics.

Brussels, Belgium — Where It Began

Brussels was the movement’s cradle. Horta’s Hôtel Tassel (1893) is widely considered the first fully realized Art Nouveau building. The Belgian variant is characterized by the dramatic “whiplash” curve — bold, undulating S-forms in iron, glass, and stone. Brussels attracted an extraordinary concentration of talent: Horta, van de Velde, and Paul Hankar all worked in the city, and their townhouses and public buildings made it an essential pilgrimage destination for architects from across Europe.

Paris and Nancy, France — The Two Poles

In France, Art Nouveau had two centers. Paris was the commercial and cultural capital, home to Bing’s gallery, Guimard’s Métro entrances, Mucha’s poster workshops, and Lalique’s jewelry ateliers. Nancy, in northeastern France, developed its own school around Émile Gallé, Louis Majorelle, and the glassmakers and furniture designers of the École de Nancy (formally established 1901). The Nancy variant tended toward more naturalistic, botanically precise forms, reflecting the region’s craft traditions and proximity to the natural world.

Vienna, Austria — Geometry and Gold

The Vienna Secession, founded in 1897, developed a variant of Art Nouveau that moved earlier and more decisively toward geometric abstraction. Where French and Belgian practitioners favored flowing curves, the Viennese — Klimt, Hoffmann, Moser, Wagner — increasingly favored rectilinear forms, checkerboard patterns, and restrained geometric ornament. The Wiener Werkstätte, founded in 1903, embodied this more disciplined approach, producing objects that look forward to Art Deco and early modernism. By around 1903, the Viennese had already begun to leave the organic whiplash behind.

Barcelona, Spain — Modernisme and National Identity

Catalan Modernisme was inseparable from the region’s drive for cultural and political distinctiveness from Castilian Spain. Barcelona’s architects — Gaudí foremost among them, alongside Lluís Domènech i Montaner (who designed over thirty buildings in Catalonia, including the Palau de la Música Catalana) — produced work that was more ornamental, more colorful, and more structurally daring than Art Nouveau elsewhere. Gaudí’s use of trencadís, parabolic arches, and nature-derived structural systems gave Catalan Modernisme an almost visionary quality that transcended the broader movement’s conventions.

Glasgow, Scotland — Restraint and Rigor

Mackintosh and his circle — including his wife Margaret Macdonald, her sister Frances Macdonald, and Herbert MacNair (collectively known as “The Four”) — developed a version of Art Nouveau defined by vertical emphasis, geometric structure, and a pale, austere color palette. The Glasgow Style influenced the Vienna Secessionists directly: when Mackintosh exhibited in Vienna in 1900, Hoffmann and his colleagues recognized a kindred spirit. Glasgow’s Art Nouveau proved that the movement’s organic impulse could be disciplined without being diminished.

Italy — Stile Liberty

Italian Art Nouveau, named after the London department store Liberty & Co. (whose fabrics were fashionable in Italy), appeared primarily in cities outside Rome: Milan, Turin, and Palermo. The style absorbed Italian Baroque exuberance, producing buildings of lavish ornament and vivid color. In Sicily, architects like the Basile family incorporated Arab-Norman and Islamic motifs, creating a uniquely Mediterranean fusion.

Germany — Jugendstil

Named after the Munich-based magazine Jugend (“Youth,” founded 1896), German Jugendstil tended toward a more structured, linear interpretation of Art Nouveau. Peter Behrens, who would later become a pioneer of industrial design, began his career in this movement. The Darmstadt Artists’ Colony (established 1899) became a center for Jugendstil architecture and design.

Part 5 — Things: Landmark Works

Architecture

Hôtel Tassel (Brussels, 1893) — Victor Horta. The building that launched Art Nouveau architecture: exposed iron structure treated as organic decoration, open floor plan, unified interior design from staircase to door handles.

Paris Métro Entrances (Paris, 1900–1913) — Hector Guimard. Cast-iron and glass canopies in organic forms, industrially produced in three standard designs. The most publicly visible Art Nouveau artifacts in the world.

Casa Batlló (Barcelona, 1904–1906) — Antoni Gaudí. A renovation of an existing apartment building transformed into a marine-organic fantasy of undulating stone, skeletal balconies, and shimmering tile mosaic.

Glasgow School of Art (Glasgow, 1897–1909) — Charles Rennie Mackintosh. A masterclass in controlled Art Nouveau: rectilinear massing, Japanese-inspired interiors, subtle organic detailing.

Austrian Postal Savings Bank (Vienna, 1904–1906) — Otto Wagner. A transitional work that bridges Art Nouveau’s decorative ambition with the stripped-down rationality of early modernism.

Decorative Arts and Design

Tiffany Lamps (New York, c. 1895–1920) — Louis Comfort Tiffany. Opalescent Favrile glass shades depicting wisteria, dragonflies, poppies, and water lilies, assembled with copper foil rather than lead came.

Gallé Cameo Glass (Nancy, 1880s–1904) — Émile Gallé. Layered, acid-etched glass vases and lamps with botanical and landscape imagery, elevating glasswork to fine art.

Lalique Jewelry (Paris, 1890s–1910s) — René Lalique. Dragonfly corsages, serpent necklaces, and orchid brooches in enamel, glass, and semi-precious stones — design-driven rather than gem-driven.

Graphic Art and Illustration

Gismonda Poster (Paris, 1895) — Alphonse Mucha. The poster that launched Mucha’s career and became an icon of Art Nouveau graphic design: tall format, idealized female figure, ornamental borders, integrated typography.

Salomé Illustrations (London, 1894) — Aubrey Beardsley. Darkly erotic black-ink drawings for Oscar Wilde’s play, demonstrating Art Nouveau’s capacity for psychological complexity and provocation.

Painting

The Kiss (Vienna, 1907–1908) — Gustav Klimt. Figurative painting dissolved into ornamental gold and geometric pattern, purchased by the Austrian government while still unfinished.

Part 6 — Decline: Why Art Nouveau Faded

Art Nouveau’s dominance was remarkably brief — barely two decades. Several factors contributed to its rapid decline.

The style’s reliance on handcrafted, labor-intensive production made Art Nouveau objects expensive. Despite the movement’s aspiration to transform everyday life, its products remained largely accessible only to wealthy patrons, echoing the same contradiction that had plagued the Arts and Crafts movement before it.

Cultural backlash set in quickly. By 1903, critics were already complaining that the style had become excessive and mannered. The English artist Walter Crane called it a “strange decorative disease.” In France, Art Nouveau acquired negative associations with foreign (Belgian and German) influence and, in some cases, with socialist politics. What had seemed fresh and revolutionary in 1895 looked overwrought by 1908.

The Vienna Secession and Glasgow Style had already pointed toward more geometric, stripped-down approaches by the early 1900s. Designers like Peter Behrens, Josef Hoffmann, and Adolf Loos (whose influential 1908 essay “Ornament and Crime” attacked decorative excess) were charting a path toward functionalism that would soon make Art Nouveau’s organic profusion seem old-fashioned.

World War I (1914–1918) delivered the final blow. The catastrophe of industrial warfare made the Belle Époque’s aesthetic confidence feel naive. The postwar world demanded something leaner, harder, and more forward-looking. Art Deco, with its geometric discipline and machine-age glamour, took Art Nouveau’s place.

Part 7 — Legacy: What Art Nouveau Left Behind

The Direct Successor: Art Deco

Art Deco, which crystallized at the 1925 Exposition Internationale des Arts Décoratifs et Industriels Modernes in Paris, inherited Art Nouveau’s commitment to total design and decorative ambition while replacing organic curves with geometric angles. Many Art Nouveau practitioners — Lalique most notably — transitioned smoothly into Art Deco production.

The Path to Modernism

Through figures like van de Velde (who laid the groundwork for the Bauhaus), Hoffmann and the Wiener Werkstätte (which anticipated modernist design production), and Wagner (whose later buildings moved toward pure functionalism), Art Nouveau fed directly into the modernist movement that would dominate the twentieth century. The movement’s insistence that designers should think across disciplines — architecture, furniture, graphics, objects — established the model of the modern designer as a generalist and systems thinker.

The 1960s Revival

In the 1960s, psychedelic poster artists in San Francisco rediscovered Art Nouveau and borrowed extensively from its visual language. The connection was not merely formal — both movements arose from dissatisfaction with mainstream culture and a desire for more “natural,” expressive ways of living. Mucha’s poster style, in particular, experienced a dramatic revival.

Contemporary Echoes

Art Nouveau’s influence persists in contemporary graphic design, fashion, interior design, and architectural ornament. Its core argument — that modernity and beauty are not opposed, that technology can serve aesthetic expression, and that design should aspire to transform the total environment rather than merely decorate isolated objects — remains compelling more than a century later.

Discussion Questions

  1. Art Nouveau aspired to the Gesamtkunstwerk — a total, unified aesthetic environment. Is this ideal achievable today? Desirable? What would a twenty-first-century Gesamtkunstwerk look like?

  2. The movement borrowed heavily from Japanese art. How should we think about this cultural exchange? Where is the line between inspiration and appropriation?

  3. Art Nouveau’s handcrafted luxury was largely accessible only to the wealthy. Does this undermine its ideals? How does this tension compare to similar tensions in contemporary design?

  4. The regional variations of Art Nouveau (Belgian, Viennese, Catalan, Scottish) were shaped by local culture and politics. Can you identify examples of contemporary design movements that are similarly tied to regional identity?

  5. Art Nouveau faded rapidly after barely two decades. What does its rise and fall tell us about how design movements work — and about the design trends we see today?
