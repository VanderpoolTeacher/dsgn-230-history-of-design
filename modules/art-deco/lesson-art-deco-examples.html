Art Deco — Key Examples

Visual Reference Guide
DSGN 230 — Survey of Design History

Architecture

Chrysler Building — William Van Alen, 1928–1930, New York, USA

The quintessential Art Deco skyscraper. Stainless steel sunburst crown with triangular windows, eagle gargoyles modeled on Chrysler radiator caps, and a lobby sheathed in African marble with painted ceiling murals by Edward Trumbull celebrating transportation and human endeavor. 1,046 feet tall — briefly the world's tallest building before the Empire State Building surpassed it.

Empire State Building — Shreve, Lamb & Harmon, 1930–1931, New York, USA

Setback massing in limestone and granite, crowned by a mooring mast (originally intended for dirigibles) that became the building's iconic spire. The Art Deco lobby features marble walls, aluminum ceiling reliefs, and a backlit mural of the building itself. 1,454 feet to antenna tip. The world's tallest building for 39 years.

Rockefeller Center — Associated Architects (Raymond Hood, lead), 1930–1939, New York, USA A 14-building Art Deco complex covering 22 acres in midtown Manhattan. Key features include Lee Lawrie's Atlas and Wisdom sculptures, the sunken plaza, the Channel Gardens, and Radio City Music Hall — whose interior, designed by Donald Deskey, is one of the most complete Art Deco environments in existence.

Radio City Music Hall — Edward Durell Stone & Donald Deskey, 1932, New York, USA The world's largest indoor theater at its opening (5,960 seats). Deskey's interior is a masterwork of Streamline Moderne: sweeping arches, geometric carpet patterns, aluminum and Bakelite fixtures, Ezra Winter's enormous lobby mural The Fountain of Youth, and the "sunrise" proscenium arch — concentric bands of light radiating outward from the stage.

Eastern Columbia Building — Claud Beelman, 1929, Los Angeles, USA A 13-story clock tower clad in turquoise and gold terra cotta tile with sunburst, zigzag, and chevron motifs. One of the finest examples of Art Deco polychrome architecture. Now residential lofts.

Palais de Chaillot — Boileau, Carlu & Azéma, 1937, Paris, France Twin curving wings with monumental sculpture and gold-inscribed poetry, facing the Eiffel Tower across the Trocadéro gardens. Built for the 1937 International Exposition, replacing an earlier Moorish-style palace.

Hoover Building — Wallis, Gilbert and Partners, 1931–1935, London, England A factory and headquarters building in white concrete with bold geometric ornamentation in green, blue, and orange faience tiles. Originally built for the Hoover Company, now a Tesco supermarket with the preserved Art Deco facade.

Marine Building — McCarter Nairne and Partners, 1930, Vancouver, Canada A 22-story office tower whose exterior and interior are encrusted with marine and transportation motifs — seahorses, crabs, ships, locomotives, aircraft — rendered in terra cotta, brass, and inlaid wood. Often called the best Art Deco building in Canada.

Bacardí Building — Esteban Rodríguez-Castells & Rafael Fernández Ruenes, 1930, Havana, Cuba Headquarters for the Bacardí rum company, featuring a stepped tower clad in local limestone and Swedish granite with terra cotta ornamentation, a bat motif (the Bacardí logo), and an interior of polychrome tiles and marble.

Miami Beach Art Deco District — Multiple architects (Hohauser, Dixon, Anis, et al.), 1923–1943, Miami Beach, USA Over 800 Art Deco and Streamline Moderne buildings forming the largest concentration of Art Deco architecture in the world. Characterized by pastel colors, nautical motifs, eyebrow overhangs, neon signage, and tropical adaptations. National Historic Landmark since 1979.

Decorative Arts & Design

Hôtel du Collectionneur — Émile-Jacques Ruhlmann (interior), Pierre Patout (architecture), 1925, Paris Exposition Ruhlmann's showcase pavilion at the 1925 Exposition. A complete Art Deco interior: macassar ebony furniture, Aubusson carpets, Jean Dupas murals, Edgar Brandt metalwork, and Jean Dunand lacquer panels, all unified by Ruhlmann's vision of refined geometric luxury.

Ruhlmann "Tardieu" Desk — Émile-Jacques Ruhlmann, c. 1929, Paris A kidney-shaped desk in macassar ebony veneer with ivory sabots (feet caps), silver-plated bronze mounts, and a single sweeping curve. Demonstrates Ruhlmann's synthesis of classical furniture-making tradition with Art Deco geometry.

Lalique "Bacchantes" Vase — René Lalique, 1927, Paris A molded opalescent glass vase featuring a frieze of nude female figures in relief, their bodies rendered in stylized, geometric repetition. One of Lalique's most iconic Art Deco designs, still produced by the Lalique company.

Lalique Glass Columns, SS Normandie — René Lalique, 1935 Twelve illuminated glass columns lining the first-class dining room of the ocean liner Normandie. Each column contained internally lit Lalique glass panels with geometric and floral motifs, creating a cathedral-like effect in the 305-foot-long room.

Jean Dunand Lacquer Panels, SS Normandie — Jean Dunand, 1935 Monumental lacquer panels in the Normandie's smoking room and other spaces, depicting stylized figures, landscapes, and geometric patterns in gold, silver, and eggshell on black lacquer. Among the most ambitious Art Deco decorative commissions ever executed.

Edgar Brandt "L'Oasis" Screen — Edgar Brandt, c. 1924, Paris A five-panel wrought-iron and bronze screen depicting a stylized fountain surrounded by flowers, scrolls, and geometric sunburst patterns. Brandt's masterpiece, bridging Art Nouveau's metalwork tradition with Art Deco's geometric vocabulary.

Cartier "Tutti Frutti" Jewelry — Cartier, 1920s–1930s, Paris Bracelets, brooches, and necklaces combining carved rubies, emeralds, and sapphires (often sourced from India) with diamonds in geometric platinum settings. The Tutti Frutti pieces epitomize Art Deco's fusion of Eastern and Western decorative traditions and its appetite for bold color contrast.

Coldspot Refrigerator — Raymond Loewy for Sears, 1934, USA A streamlined household refrigerator with chrome hardware, rounded edges, and clean horizontal lines. One of the first consumer products to be "styled" by an industrial designer, it tripled Sears' refrigerator sales and demonstrated that Art Deco aesthetics could sell mass-market goods.

Graphic Design & Posters

Étoile du Nord — A.M. Cassandre, 1927, Paris Poster for the Paris–Amsterdam express railway. Railway tracks converge in dramatic one-point perspective toward a glowing star, simplified to pure geometry. Bold typography, limited color palette (blue, black, gold). Among the most influential travel posters ever made.

Normandie — A.M. Cassandre, 1935, Paris The ocean liner's black prow towers above the viewer in extreme low-angle perspective, rendered as a monolithic geometric form. Dramatic scale, stark contrast, and minimal palette create an image of overwhelming industrial power and luxury.

Dubonnet — A.M. Cassandre, 1932, Paris A three-panel sequential poster showing a figure progressively drinking Dubonnet as the word gradually fills with color. Innovative narrative structure, bold geometric figure, and witty integration of text and image. A landmark in advertising design.

Harper's Bazaar Covers — Erté, 1915–1937, Paris/New York Over 200 covers featuring Erté's impossibly elegant, elongated female figures in elaborate costumes and settings. Metallic gouache on black backgrounds, geometric compositions, and an Art Deco sensibility that defined the magazine's visual identity for two decades.

Metropolis Film Poster — Heinz Schulz-Neudamm, 1927, Berlin Poster for Fritz Lang's expressionist film, featuring the Maschinenmensch (Machine-Human) against a geometric cityscape of towering skyscrapers. One of the most valuable film posters in existence (a copy sold for $690,000 in 2005), its fusion of technology, geometry, and futurism is pure Art Deco.

Painting, Sculpture & Murals

Autoportrait (Tamara in the Green Bugatti) — Tamara de Lempicka, 1929 Self-portrait at the wheel of a green Bugatti racing car, rendered in polished Cubist geometry. Originally commissioned as a cover for the German fashion magazine Die Dame. De Lempicka's helmet and scarf stream in the wind; every surface gleams. The defining image of Art Deco painting.

Aspects of Negro Life — Aaron Douglas, 1934, Schomburg Center, New York Four monumental oil-on-canvas murals: The Negro in an African Setting, An Idyll of the Deep South, From Slavery Through Reconstruction, and Song of the Towers. Flat geometric silhouettes, concentric arcs of light, muted palette. The visual masterpiece of the Harlem Renaissance and one of Art Deco's most important mural cycles.

Atlas — Lee Lawrie, 1937, Rockefeller Center, New York A 15-foot bronze statue of the Titan Atlas supporting an armillary sphere (celestial rings) on his shoulders. Geometric musculature, stylized features, and an open-frame sphere that plays dramatically with light and sky. One of Art Deco's most photographed sculptures.

Prometheus — Paul Manship, 1934, Rockefeller Center, New York A gilded bronze Titan descending with fire, set in the sunken plaza against a backdrop of gold leaf and a quotation from Aeschylus. Manship's sleek, stylized figuration — muscular but geometricized — exemplifies Art Deco sculpture's balance between classicism and modernism.

Spirit of Transportation — Edward Trumbull, 1930, Chrysler Building, New York Painted ceiling mural in the Chrysler Building lobby, approximately 110 by 76 feet, depicting the Assembly Line, airplane propellers, the Chrysler Building itself, and heroic figures of labor and industry — all in the compressed, angular perspective characteristic of Art Deco muralism.

The Conquest of the Sky — Jean Dupas, 1935, SS Normandie Large-scale lacquer and gold-leaf panel in the Normandie's first-class grand salon, depicting mythological and historical scenes of flight and navigation in a flat, highly stylized Art Deco manner. Among the most important decorative commissions of the 1930s.

Industrial & Product Design

Pennsylvania Railroad S1 Locomotive — Raymond Loewy, 1939, USA A streamlined steam locomotive with a bullet-shaped nose shroud, horizontal speed lines, and a smooth, aerodynamic shell concealing the mechanical works beneath. Exhibited at the 1939 New York World's Fair. A monument to Streamline Moderne industrial design.

Chrysler Airflow — Carl Breer, 1934, USA One of the first mass-produced cars designed using wind-tunnel testing and aerodynamic principles. Its rounded, streamlined body — a radical departure from the boxy cars of the period — was commercially unsuccessful but enormously influential, pointing toward the future of automotive design.

Normandie Ocean Liner — Vladimir Yourkevitch (hull), multiple designers (interiors), 1935, France The most lavish ocean liner ever built: 1,028 feet long, Art Deco interiors by Lalique, Dunand, Dupas, Ruhlmann, and others. The first-class dining room was longer than the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles. The ship itself was a floating museum of Art Deco design at its most ambitious.

Walter Dorwin Teague Kodak Bantam Special Camera — Walter Dorwin Teague, 1936, USA A compact camera with a black enamel body, chrome speed lines, and geometric stepped detailing. A consumer product designed as a miniature Art Deco object, demonstrating that Streamline Moderne could operate at pocket scale.
