Historical Context

The Screen Became the Canvas

Three Eras · Web · Mobile · AI

From Page to Surface to System

When postmodernism ran out of rebellion in the late 1980s, the next generation of designers found a genuinely new medium waiting for them: the personal computer and, within a decade, the browser. For the first time in design history, the primary surface was luminous, scrollable, interactive, and shared. A page of paper is a finished object; a web page is a running program. That difference is the pivot on which the last thirty-five years of design history turn.

The era divides roughly into three overlapping generations. The web generation (c. 1990–2005) invented the vocabulary of the browser as a design surface — nearly from scratch, and often clumsily. The mobile and platform generation (c. 2005–2020) learned to think in systems rather than pages, treating the Android and iOS home screens as the dominant design surfaces on the planet. The AI generation (c. 2020–present) is still in motion: generative models for text, image, and code are changing what "making a design" even means, and we do not yet know how the story ends.

The Browser Arrives
Tim Berners-Lee's first web page goes live at CERN in 1991. Mosaic brings inline images in 1993. By 1996 the commercial web is a business, and its design conventions are being invented in real time.
User-Centered Design
Don Norman's The Design of Everyday Things (1988) provides the vocabulary. Jakob Nielsen and Steve Krug turn it into a practice. By 2005 "UX" is a job title, not an argument.
The iPhone Moment
January 2007. A touch surface the size of a paperback becomes the primary computing device on the planet within a decade. Interface design becomes mobile design becomes platform design.
Design Systems Era
Material Design (2014), Airbnb DLS (2016), Atomic Design (2013), Figma (2016). Design work moves from drawing pages to maintaining systems. The component, not the composition, is the atom.
Inclusive Design
Microsoft's Inclusive Design Toolkit (2016) reframes accessibility as inclusion and inclusion as method. Kat Holmes's Mismatch (2018) provides the theory. Accessibility stops being a feature and becomes a baseline.
The AI Question
GPT-3 (2020), DALL-E 2 (2022), ChatGPT (2022), Midjourney (2022). Generative models become tools, collaborators, and threats. The profession is actively arguing about what happens next.

Defining Characteristics

Screen-native: designs live on luminous, interactive surfaces whose shape changes per device, not fixed pages. Responsive and fluid: Ethan Marcotte's 2010 methodology teaches the web to bend around unknown viewports. Systems over artifacts: the design system, not the mockup, becomes the primary deliverable. User-centered: research, testing, and iteration replace taste and intuition as the basis for design decisions. Accessible by default: WCAG compliance, screen-reader support, and keyboard navigation become minimum expectations for public work. Increasingly data-driven: analytics, A/B tests, and eventually machine-learning models shape what users see. Generative: by the mid-2020s, parts of the design output are being produced by models the designer prompts rather than drawn by the designer directly.

Think About It

Postmodernism rebelled against a modernist establishment. The digital era never had a single establishment to rebel against — instead it has been continuously remaking itself, tool by tool, platform by platform. Is that continuous reinvention a kind of health, or a kind of exhaustion? What would it mean for contemporary design to slow down?

Observe

These twelve works span thirty-odd years and three distinct technological eras. What holds them together as a single design tradition? Look for the shared commitment to the screen as a surface, the recurring question of who the user is, and the gradual shift from making single artifacts to maintaining running systems.

Key Figures

The People of the Digital Era

Tim Berners-Lee
b. 1955 · CERN / MIT

Inventor of the Web

Proposed the World Wide Web at CERN in 1989 and published the first web page in 1991. Designed HTTP, URLs, and HTML as open standards rather than proprietary protocols — the single most consequential design decision of the digital era. Later founded the W3C to keep the web's foundations open.

Don Norman
b. 1935 · UC San Diego / Apple
Web
Click to flip
Cognitive Scientist & Design Theorist

His The Design of Everyday Things (1988) is the foundational text of user-centered design. Coined the design use of the term "affordance." Coined the term "user experience" while at Apple in the early 1990s. The Nielsen Norman Group (1998) became the consultancy that carried UX into industry.

Jakob Nielsen
b. 1957 · Nielsen Norman Group
Web
Click to flip
Usability Consultant

The relentless advocate for usability over visual sophistication. His site useit.com was deliberately ugly as a demonstration that fast-loading, legible pages respect the user's time. Designing Web Usability (2000) codified his method. Famously argued that the web needed plainness, not decoration — a position that was controversial at the time and quietly correct in retrospect.

Susan Kare
b. 1954 · Apple / General Magic
Web
Click to flip
Interface Icon Designer

Drew the original Macintosh icons in 1983: the trash can, the dogcow, the watch cursor, the smiling Mac. Her pixel-level work taught a generation of designers that an icon was a piece of typography rather than a piece of illustration. The visual grammar she established — friendly, legible, small — is still the default for interface iconography four decades later.

Jeffrey Zeldman
b. 1955 · A List Apart / Happy Cog
Web
Click to flip
Web Standards Advocate

Founded A List Apart in 1998 and led the Web Standards Project. His 2003 book Designing With Web Standards taught the professional web to use semantic HTML and CSS instead of the table hacks that dominated the 1990s. One of the reasons the modern web works at all.

Ethan Marcotte
b. 1977 · Boston
Web · Mobile
Click to flip
Designer & Writer

Published "Responsive Web Design" in A List Apart in 2010, naming and specifying the methodology (fluid grids + flexible images + media queries) that taught the web to work on any screen. The 2011 book, Responsive Web Design, became the practical handbook for the entire field.

Jony Ive
b. 1967 · Apple / LoveFrom
Mobile
Click to flip
Industrial & Interface Designer

Led design at Apple from 1996 to 2019. His hardware work (iMac, iPod, iPhone, iPad) shaped consumer electronics worldwide. His 2013 iOS 7 redesign retired skeuomorphism and established the flat, typographically-driven visual language that is now the default across mobile platforms.

Matias Duarte
b. 1973 · Google
Mobile
Click to flip
Platform Design Lead

Led the design of webOS at Palm before moving to Google in 2010. As VP of Design at Google, he oversaw Material Design (2014), the first comprehensive, publicly documented platform design system from a major tech company. Material's "virtual paper" metaphor and its open documentation set the template every subsequent platform has followed.

Kat Holmes
Microsoft / Google / Salesforce
Mobile
Click to flip
Inclusive Design Leader

Led the creation of Microsoft's Inclusive Design Toolkit (2016), which reframed accessibility as inclusion and inclusion as a design methodology. Her 2018 book Mismatch: How Inclusion Shapes Design is the theoretical companion. Argues that disability is a mismatch between a person and their environment, not a property of the person.

Brad Frost
b. 1986 · Pittsburgh
Mobile
Click to flip
Front-End Designer & Writer

Published "Atomic Design" as an article in 2013 and a book in 2016. His atoms / molecules / organisms / templates / pages hierarchy gave the web industry a shared vocabulary for component-based interface design at exactly the moment React and Vue were converging on the same ideas from engineering.

Julie Zhuo
b. 1983 · Facebook / Sundial
Mobile
Click to flip
Design Manager & Author

Became VP of Design at Facebook in her late twenties, eventually leading a team of hundreds. Her 2019 book The Making of a Manager is partly a how-to guide and partly a record of how design management became a distinct discipline as design teams scaled from small craft groups into full organizations.

Refik Anadol
b. 1985 · Istanbul / Los Angeles
AI
Click to flip
Media Artist

Works with machine-learning models as aesthetic material. His Unsupervised, installed at MoMA from November 2022 through March 2023, trained on MoMA's own collection metadata to produce continuously generated "machine hallucinations" on a wall-sized screen. The first major museum presentation to assign aesthetic authorship to a system as much as to the artist using it.

Ideas & Principles

What Makes Design "Digital"

The operating assumptions of contemporary practice

Unlike postmodernism, the digital era has never had a single manifesto. Its principles accumulated incrementally, argued out in blog posts and conference talks and platform documentation. These are the ideas that almost every serious contemporary design practice now takes as given.

User-Centered
Research, testing, iteration
Click to flip
Methodology

The assumption that design decisions should be grounded in research about actual users rather than the designer's taste. User research, usability testing, analytics, A/B tests, and iteration on feedback are the standard tools. Don Norman articulated the theory in 1988; Jakob Nielsen and Steve Krug turned it into daily practice through the 1990s and 2000s.

Responsive & Fluid
Marcotte, 2010
Click to flip
Technical Method

The web's native condition is fluidity: the designer does not know the shape of the user's screen. Responsive design, named by Ethan Marcotte in 2010, combines fluid grids, flexible images, and media queries to produce layouts that bend gracefully from a phone to a desktop monitor. By 2015 it was the default assumption of the field.

Systems Over Artifacts
Material Design, Atomic Design
Click to flip
Deliverable Shift

The primary deliverable of serious contemporary design work is no longer a mockup of a page but a design system: a documented, versioned, reusable library of components, tokens, and rules. The system is maintained continuously, not shipped once. The page is a configuration of the system, not the thing the designer made.

Inclusive by Default
Holmes & Microsoft, 2016
Click to flip
Ethical Baseline

Accessibility is not a feature to be added; it is a property of the design from the start. Kat Holmes's reframing of disability as a "mismatch" between person and environment (rather than a property of the person) shifted the argument from accommodation to design methodology. Captions help more than deaf users; ramps help more than wheelchair users.

Performance as Craft
Nielsen, Krug, and after
Click to flip
Respect for the User

Fast-loading pages and responsive interactions are not just technical goals but design goals. The user's time, battery, data plan, and attention are finite and valuable, and a bloated interface is a form of disrespect. This principle runs from Nielsen's 1990s polemics through GOV.UK's plain-and-fast aesthetic to Low-tech Magazine's solar-powered server.

Sustainability
The environmental cost of pixels
Click to flip
Emerging Principle

The physical infrastructure of the web — servers, network, batteries — costs energy, and bloated interfaces scale that cost globally. Gerry McGovern's World Wide Waste (2020), Low-tech Magazine's solar-powered site, and the growing "sustainable web" movement make the argument that pixel-level decisions have planetary consequences. This principle is still being fought for.

Data-Driven
Analytics, A/B tests, ML
Click to flip
Decision-Making

Contemporary product teams increasingly settle design arguments with data: click-through rates, retention curves, A/B-tested button copy, machine-learned personalization. The upside is that bad assumptions get caught. The downside — what Mike Monteiro calls "metrics over ethics" — is that designs optimized for short-term engagement can produce dark patterns and addictive loops the team never explicitly chose.

Generative
The AI turn, 2020–
Click to flip
New Material

With GPT-3, DALL-E, Midjourney, Stable Diffusion, and ChatGPT, designers have begun working with generative models as a design material. Parts of the output are produced by systems the designer prompts rather than drawn directly. The profession is actively debating what this means for authorship, labor, training data, and the environmental cost of the compute involved.

Think About It

Each of these principles began as an argument someone was making against the current consensus, and each eventually became the new consensus. Which principles on this page are still being argued for? Which have become invisible because they are no longer contested? And which, on reflection, might be ready for their own rebellion?
