Arts & Crafts — Examples List

A reference catalog of iconic works
c. 1880–1910

A companion to the main lesson: buildings, textiles, books, furniture, metalwork, and institutions that define the Arts and Crafts Movement. Organized by category. Dates are approximate where records disagree.

Architecture and Interiors

Red House — Philip Webb for William and Jane Morris, Bexleyheath, Kent, 1860. Red-brick, steeply pitched roofs, asymmetrical L-plan, exposed construction. The interiors were decorated by Morris, Webb, Burne-Jones, Rossetti, and Ford Madox Brown. The prototype of the Arts and Crafts "total environment" and the immediate occasion for the founding of Morris & Co.

Standen — Philip Webb, near East Grinstead, Sussex, 1891–1894. The most completely preserved of Webb’s country houses, with Morris wallpapers, textiles, and furniture throughout. Now owned by the National Trust.

Clouds — Philip Webb, Wiltshire, 1881–1891, for the Wyndham family. A huge country house that put the movement’s vocabulary to aristocratic scale. Partially destroyed by fire in 1889, rebuilt, and dismantled in the 1930s.

The Glasgow School of Art — Charles Rennie Mackintosh, Glasgow, 1897–1909. Stone, iron, glass, and extraordinary interiors. Pushes Arts and Crafts toward an austere proto-modernism. Tragically damaged by fires in 2014 and 2018.

Hill House — Charles Rennie Mackintosh, Helensburgh, Scotland, 1902. Mackintosh’s finest surviving domestic work, designed for the publisher Walter Blackie. Every detail, from the furniture to the light fittings, was drawn by Mackintosh and his wife Margaret Macdonald.

Blackwell — Baillie Scott, Bowness-on-Windermere, 1898–1900. An Arts and Crafts holiday house above Lake Windermere, with carved oak, leaded glass, and plasterwork all designed by Scott. Now open to the public.

The Orchard — C. F. A. Voysey, Chorleywood, 1899. Voysey’s own house, and the archetype of the compact Arts and Crafts villa: whitewashed rendered walls, low eaves, horizontal windows, and restrained interiors. Voysey’s work was published widely in Europe and deeply influenced early modernism.

The Gamble House — Greene and Greene, Pasadena, California, 1908–1909. The masterpiece of the California Arts and Crafts bungalow: teak and redwood joinery, Japanese-influenced bracketing, and custom stained glass by the Sturdy-Lange studio. One of the most exquisite pieces of American domestic architecture.

The Roycroft Campus — East Aurora, New York, from 1895. A complex of buildings — print shop, copper shop, chapel, inn — built to house Elbert Hubbard’s craft community. Now a National Historic Landmark.

Furniture

The Sussex chair — Morris & Co., from the mid-1860s. A simple ebonized chair with turned legs and a rush seat, based on a traditional country pattern. It became one of the firm’s most popular objects and a symbol of unpretentious craft furniture.

Morris chair — Morris & Co. and many imitators, from c. 1866. An adjustable-back oak armchair with a reclining mechanism, originally designed by Philip Webb. Gustav Stickley’s later American version made it synonymous with Craftsman furniture in the United States.

Ernest Gimson’s ladder-back chair — Cotswold school, 1890s–1910s. A rush-seated oak or ash chair in the style of traditional English country furniture, revived and refined by Gimson and his workshop at Sapperton.

Sidney Barnsley’s oak dressers — Cotswold school, c. 1900. Massive, austere, pegged oak casework that became the template for twentieth-century English craft furniture.

Gustav Stickley’s Craftsman oak library table — Craftsman Workshops, Syracuse, from 1901. The signature American Arts and Crafts piece: quartersawn oak, exposed through-tenons, hand-hammered copper hardware.

Limbert furniture — Charles P. Limbert, Grand Rapids, Michigan, from 1902. A slightly more ornate American Craftsman line, with cut-out patterns and restrained inlay.

Textiles, Wallpaper, and Embroidery

Trellis — William Morris, 1864. Morris’s first wallpaper design, with flowers and thorns by Morris and birds by Philip Webb. Based on the rose trellis at the Red House garden.

Daisy — William Morris, 1864. The second Morris wallpaper, and the first to go into commercial production. A scatter of stylized meadow flowers on a plain ground.

Acanthus — William Morris, 1875. A densely worked wallpaper of curling acanthus leaves, printed from many blocks. Considered one of Morris’s technical high points.

Willow Bough — William Morris, 1887. The best-loved Morris wallpaper, and still in continuous production. A calm, all-over pattern of willow leaves on slender branches.

Strawberry Thief — William Morris, 1883. A printed cotton recording the thrushes that stole strawberries from the beds at Kelmscott Manor. Morris printed it using indigo-discharge, a technique he revived after years of experimentation.

Honeysuckle — William Morris, 1876. Printed cotton with an intricate, densely woven pattern. Often considered his most accomplished textile design.

Bullerswood Carpet — William Morris and John Henry Dearle, 1889. A large hand-knotted wool carpet designed for a Kentish country house. Now in the V&A.

Cray — William Morris, 1884. A bold, large-scale printed cotton in rich reds, greens, and blues, named for the River Cray. One of the most ambitious of Morris’s fabrics.

May Morris embroidered hangings — Morris & Co., from 1885. May Morris led the firm’s embroidery department from age 23 and produced major ecclesiastical and domestic commissions in her own right.

Books and Typography

The Kelmscott Chaucer — Kelmscott Press, 1896. The masterpiece of the private-press movement. 556 pages, 87 illustrations by Burne-Jones, fourteen large borders, eighteen frames, twenty-six large initial words, all printed by hand on hand-made paper. Only 425 paper and 13 vellum copies were produced.

The Nature of Gothic (Kelmscott Press edition) — 1892. Morris reprinted his favorite Ruskin chapter with his own introduction. An explicit statement of the movement’s intellectual debt.

The Kelmscott typefaces — Golden (1890), Troy (1891), and Chaucer (1892), all designed by Morris based on fifteenth-century Venetian and German models. Their revival of legible, traditional letterforms shaped twentieth-century book typography.

News from Nowhere — William Morris, 1890 (Kelmscott Press edition 1892). Morris’s utopian novel imagining a post-industrial England. The Kelmscott frontispiece, showing Kelmscott Manor, is one of the most reproduced Arts and Crafts images.

The Doves Press Bible — Doves Press, 1903–1905. A five-volume Bible printed by T. J. Cobden-Sanderson and Emery Walker at the Doves Press in Hammersmith. Austere where Kelmscott was ornate, it showed that Arts and Crafts typography could also look forward to modernism.

Roycroft books — from 1895. Elbert Hubbard’s Roycroft community produced leather-bound, hand-decorated books in large enough numbers to reach a mass American audience. Their editions of Ruskin and Emerson were especially popular.

Metalwork, Silver, and Jewelry

C. R. Ashbee decanter — Guild of Handicraft, c. 1904–1905. Silver-mounted green glass with hand-chased detailing. One of the signature objects of the guild movement.

Ashbee’s "muffin dish" — Guild of Handicraft, c. 1900. Hammered silver with a handle of coiled wire and turquoise cabochons. A study in quiet, un-showy refinement.

Liberty Cymric silver — Liberty & Co., from 1899. Designed primarily by Archibald Knox, a Manx designer whose Celtic-inflected patterns gave Liberty a distinctive range of commercially produced Arts and Crafts silver.

Liberty Tudric pewter — Liberty & Co., from 1901. The pewter counterpart to Cymric. Broader distribution meant that for the first time, well-designed metalwork in the Arts and Crafts idiom was available at middle-class prices.

Roycroft copper — Roycroft community, from c. 1908. Hand-hammered copper lamps, vases, bookends, and desk sets. Karl Kipp and Walter Jennings were the guiding designers.

Dirk Van Erp copper lamps — San Francisco, from 1908. Hammered-copper table lamps with mica shades that became iconic in California Arts and Crafts interiors.

Books of Arguments

The Nature of Gothic — John Ruskin, 1853 (chapter of The Stones of Venice vol. II). The founding text of the movement. Argues that Gothic architecture is humane because it lets ordinary masons think.

Unto This Last — John Ruskin, 1860. Four essays on political economy that reframed design criticism as social criticism. A book Morris and later Gandhi both credited with changing their lives.

Hopes and Fears for Art — William Morris, 1882. Collected lectures, including "The Lesser Arts" and "The Beauty of Life," where Morris lays out the central arguments of the movement.

News from Nowhere — William Morris, 1890. Utopian novel in which a man falls asleep in nineteenth-century London and wakes up in a future England where factories have been dismantled and people make things because they want to.

The Claims of Decorative Art — Walter Crane, 1892. Crane’s most influential prose book, part manifesto and part design theory.

The Bases of Design and Line and Form — Walter Crane, 1898 and 1900. The movement’s practical textbooks, used in design schools in Britain and the United States for decades.

The Craftsman (magazine) — Gustav Stickley, 1901–1916. A monthly magazine with furniture plans, house designs, essays on simple living, and articles on craft. The main channel for Arts and Crafts ideas in the United States.

Institutions, Guilds, and Exhibitions

Morris, Marshall, Faulkner & Co. / Morris & Co. — founded 1861, reorganized 1875. The movement’s most influential workshop and commercial vehicle. Still in existence as a wallpaper and textile company after 160 years.

Century Guild — founded by Arthur Mackmurdo, 1882. The first English design guild organized on medieval principles, and an important stepping stone to the full Arts and Crafts Movement.

Art Workers’ Guild — founded 1884. A professional society for designers, architects, and craftspeople that still exists today at 6 Queen Square in London.

Arts and Crafts Exhibition Society — founded 1887. The society that held the 1888 exhibition and gave the movement its name.

Guild of Handicraft — C. R. Ashbee, founded in London 1888, relocated to Chipping Campden in 1902, dissolved in 1907. The most famous of the craft cooperatives.

Kelmscott Press — William Morris, founded 1891, closed 1898. Fifty-three books produced in seven years. The founding model for the twentieth-century private-press movement.

Birmingham Guild of Handicraft — founded 1890. A smaller but longer-lived parallel to Ashbee’s London guild.

The Cotswold School — Ernest Gimson, Ernest Barnsley, and Sidney Barnsley at Sapperton, from 1893. A workshop-and-house community that produced some of the finest English craft furniture.

The Craftsman Workshops — Gustav Stickley, Syracuse, New York, from 1898. The dominant American workshop of the Craftsman movement.

The Roycroft Community — Elbert Hubbard, East Aurora, New York, from 1895. Printing, furniture, leatherwork, metalwork, and an influential magazine.

Wiener Werkstätte — Vienna, founded 1903 by Josef Hoffmann and Koloman Moser, explicitly modeled on Ashbee’s Guild of Handicraft. One of the most important direct descendants of the British movement on the Continent.
