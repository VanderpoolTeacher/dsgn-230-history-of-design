Early Modernism & the Avant-Garde — Key Examples

Visual Reference Guide
DSGN 230 — Survey of Design History

Cubist Sculpture & Design (c. 1910–1920)

Sculpture & Objects

Guitar — Pablo Picasso, 1912–1914, Paris, France Constructed from sheet metal and wire, this is one of the first sculptures made by assembling flat materials rather than carving or modeling. The guitar’s sound hole projects outward as a cylinder, reversing the expected concavity. Now in the Museum of Modern Art, New York.

Head of a Woman (Fernande) — Pablo Picasso, 1909, Paris, France Bronze head with fractured, faceted planes replacing smooth contours. One of the earliest three-dimensional translations of Cubist painting principles. Exists in multiple bronze casts; examples at the Metropolitan Museum of Art and Centre Pompidou.

Glass of Absinthe — Pablo Picasso, 1914, Paris, France Painted bronze sculpture of a glass topped with a real absinthe spoon and a cast sugar cube. Six casts exist, each painted differently — demonstrating that identical forms can carry different identities through surface treatment. Museum of Modern Art, New York.

Woman Walking — Alexander Archipenko, 1912, Paris, France Figure composed of concavities and voids where classical sculpture would place convex masses. The torso is a hollow, the head an absence. Archipenko treated empty space as a positive sculptural element.

Médrano II — Alexander Archipenko, 1913 (reconstructed 1957), Paris, France Polychromed assemblage of wood, metal, and glass depicting a circus figure. Combines painting, sculpture, and collage in a single three-dimensional object. Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum, New York.

Man with a Guitar — Jacques Lipchitz, 1915, Paris, France Interlocking stone planes compressed into a vertical figure. The guitar and body merge into an architectural arrangement of geometric volumes. Museum of Modern Art, New York.

Graphic Design & Typography

Cubist Collages — Pablo Picasso and Georges Braque, 1912–1914, Paris, France Picasso’s Still Life with Chair Caning (1912) and Braque’s papier collé works introduced foreign materials — newspaper, wallpaper, oilcloth — into pictorial compositions. These collages are the direct ancestors of photomontage, mixed-media graphic design, and the entire tradition of combining found imagery with hand-drawn elements.

Futurist Typography — Zang Tumb Tumb — F. T. Marinetti, 1914, Milan, Italy Though Futurist rather than strictly Cubist, Marinetti’s “words-in-freedom” typography — exploding text across pages in varying sizes, weights, and orientations — was inspired by the same impulse to shatter conventional pictorial space. Profoundly influenced twentieth-century experimental typography.

Constructivism (c. 1913–1930s)

Sculpture & Architecture

Monument to the Third International (Model) — Vladimir Tatlin, 1919–1920, Petrograd (St. Petersburg), Russia A spiraling double-helix tower of iron and glass, planned at 400 meters tall — taller than the Eiffel Tower. Interior volumes (cube, pyramid, cylinder) would rotate at different speeds. Never built, but its model became the icon of Constructivist ambition. Declared that sculpture, architecture, and political statement could be one thing.

Corner Counter-Relief — Vladimir Tatlin, 1914–1915, Moscow, Russia Metal, wire, and wood assemblage suspended across a room corner by cables. Eliminated the pedestal, the wall, and the floor as conventional supports. The room’s architecture becomes part of the work.

Linear Construction in Space No. 1 — Naum Gabo, 1942–1943 (concept from 1920s), originally Russia / later England Transparent Perspex sheets strung with nylon thread, making space itself visible. The material is a scaffold; the subject is the emptiness inside. Tate Modern, London.

Kinetic Construction (Standing Wave) — Naum Gabo, 1919–1920, Moscow, Russia A single metal rod vibrated by a motor to create the illusion of a standing wave — one of the first kinetic sculptures. Demonstrated the Realistic Manifesto’s claim that art should engage time and movement. Tate Modern, London.

Graphic Design & Typography

Beat the Whites with the Red Wedge — El Lissitzky, 1919, Russia Propaganda poster using pure geometric forms — a red triangle piercing a white circle — to communicate a political message without representational imagery. One of the most reproduced examples of how abstract form can carry specific meaning. Foundational to modern poster design.

Proun Compositions — El Lissitzky, 1919–1927, Russia / Germany Spatial compositions that existed as paintings, prints, and eventually room-sized environments. The Proun Room (1923) at the Greater Berlin Art Exhibition placed geometric forms on walls, floor, and ceiling, dissolving the distinction between artwork and architecture. A direct precursor to exhibition design as a discipline.

Books (Please)! In All Branches of Knowledge — Alexander Rodchenko, 1924, Moscow, Russia Photomontage poster for the Lengiz publishing house, featuring Lilya Brik shouting through cupped hands. Bold diagonal composition, limited color palette (red, black, white), sans-serif typography. One of the most iconic images in graphic design history.

About This (Pro Eto) Book Cover — Alexander Rodchenko, 1923, Moscow, Russia Photomontage book cover for Vladimir Mayakovsky’s poem. Combined photographs, geometric framing, and constructivist typography into a unified composition that defined an era of Soviet book design.

Workers’ Club — Alexander Rodchenko, 1925, Paris, France (exhibited) Complete interior design for a workers’ reading room, shown at the 1925 Paris Exposition. Geometric furniture in red, gray, and black; folding chairs; movable screens. Demonstrated that Constructivist principles could shape everyday environments, not just gallery objects.

Dada & the Readymade (c. 1916–1924)

Objects & Sculpture

Fountain — Marcel Duchamp (as “R. Mutt”), 1917, New York, USA A porcelain urinal turned on its back, signed, and submitted to an open exhibition. Rejected by the hanging committee despite the show’s stated policy of accepting all work. The original is lost; authorized replicas exist in major museums worldwide including the Tate, the Centre Pompidou, and the San Francisco Museum of Modern Art. The most discussed artwork of the twentieth century.

Bicycle Wheel — Marcel Duchamp, 1913, Paris, France A bicycle wheel mounted upside-down on a kitchen stool. Duchamp later called it his first readymade, though the term came later. Not selected for any symbolic meaning — Duchamp said he simply enjoyed watching it spin. The original is lost; a 1951 replica is in the Museum of Modern Art, New York.

Bottle Rack (Porte-bouteilles) — Marcel Duchamp, 1914, Paris, France A galvanized-iron bottle-drying rack purchased from a Paris department store. No alteration, no inscription (at first). The original was discarded; replicas exist. Demonstrates the readymade at its purest — an industrial object displaced into an art context with nothing added.

Gift (Cadeau) — Man Ray, 1921, Paris, France A flatiron with a row of fourteen tacks glued to its smooth surface. Transforms a domestic tool into something dangerous — it cannot perform its intended function without destroying the fabric it touches. Replicas in multiple collections.

Indestructible Object (Object to Be Destroyed) — Man Ray, 1923 (reconstructed 1964), Paris, France A metronome with a photograph of an eye clipped to its arm. The original was indeed destroyed by viewers at an exhibition in 1957. Man Ray created an edition of replicas titled Indestructible Object. Museum of Modern Art, New York.

Environments

Merzbau — Kurt Schwitters, begun 1923, Hanover, Germany An ongoing, room-filling sculptural environment that consumed Schwitters’s studio over more than a decade. Built from found materials — wood scraps, newspapers, personal artifacts, gifts from friends — encrusting columns and grottos that grew organically. Destroyed in Allied bombing in 1943. Schwitters began two further Merzbau in Norway and England, both unfinished. Anticipated installation art, site-specific sculpture, and the concept of art as ongoing process rather than finished object.

Graphic Design & Photomontage

Cut with the Kitchen Knife Dada Through the Last Weimar Beer-Belly Cultural Epoch of Germany — Hannah Höch, 1919–1920, Berlin, Germany Large-scale photomontage combining press photographs of politicians, athletes, dancers, machines, and text fragments into a chaotic, politically charged composition. Demonstrates photomontage’s power to create meaning through juxtaposition. Nationalgalerie, Berlin.

Dada Heads — Hannah Höch, 1920, Berlin, Germany Assemblage portraits combining sculpted and found objects. Her Dada Dolls subverted conventional representations of femininity using craft materials — a strategy that anticipated feminist art by decades.

Merz Magazine — Kurt Schwitters, 1923–1932, Hanover, Germany Schwitters’s own publication featuring experimental typography, collage layouts, and contributions from Constructivists, Dadaists, and De Stijl artists. The magazine operated at the intersection of Dada chaos and Constructivist order, demonstrating that both impulses could coexist on a printed page.

Theo van Doesburg & Schwitters Dada Performances — 1922–1923, Netherlands Van Doesburg (founder of De Stijl) and Schwitters toured the Netherlands together, performing Dada sound poetry and typographic experiments. The collaboration illustrates how closely intertwined these supposedly opposing movements actually were.

Surrealist Objects (c. 1924–1940s)

Objects & Sculpture

Object (Le Déjeuner en fourrure) — Meret Oppenheim, 1936, Paris, France Teacup, saucer, and spoon lined with Chinese gazelle fur. Exploits the sensory gap between visual expectation (porcelain) and tactile reality (fur against lips). One of the most famous Surrealist objects. Museum of Modern Art, New York.

Suspended Ball (Boule suspendue) — Alberto Giacometti, 1930–1931, Paris, France A cleft sphere hanging by a wire above a crescent form in an open cage. The ball can swing to slide along the crescent but never makes full contact — generating perpetual tension through near-touch. Fondation Giacometti, Paris.

The Palace at 4 a.m. — Alberto Giacometti, 1932, Paris, France Open wooden framework enclosing fragile figures and objects. Giacometti described it as a reconstruction of a period of his life, experienced as a dream. Museum of Modern Art, New York.

Lobster Telephone (Aphrodisiac Telephone) — Salvador Dalí, 1936, Paris, France A functioning telephone with a plaster lobster replacing the handset. Both elements are real and familiar; their combination produces uncanny humor. Multiple versions exist; examples at the Tate, the Walker Art Center, and the Dalí Theatre-Museum.

Mae West Lips Sofa — Salvador Dalí (with Edward James), 1937, Paris, France / England Wooden sofa upholstered in shocking pink felt, shaped as Mae West’s lips. Turned a face into furniture — an object that is simultaneously functional, representational, and absurd. Anticipated Pop Art’s interest in celebrity and mass culture by two decades. Multiple versions; original at the Dalí Theatre-Museum, Figueres.

Spoon Woman — Alberto Giacometti, 1927, Paris, France Bronze figure whose torso takes the form of an African spoon, merging Surrealist interest in the unconscious with the influence of non-Western art. Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum, New York.

Design Influence

Surrealist Window Displays — Salvador Dalí, 1939, New York, USA Dalí designed window displays for Bonwit Teller department store on Fifth Avenue featuring a fur-lined bathtub and a wax mannequin climbing into a bed. When the store altered his designs, Dalí pushed the bathtub through the plate-glass window. The incident demonstrated Surrealism’s growing influence on commercial display and retail design — and the tension between artistic vision and commercial compromise.

Schiaparelli × Dalí Collaborations — Elsa Schiaparelli and Salvador Dalí, 1936–1937, Paris, France Fashion designer Schiaparelli collaborated with Dalí to produce Surrealist clothing: the Lobster Dress (a white silk gown with a painted lobster), the Shoe Hat, and the Skeleton Dress. These collaborations represent one of the earliest fusions of avant-garde art and high fashion — a model that became standard practice in luxury fashion.

Abstract Expressionist Sculpture (c. 1940s–1960s)

Sculpture

Cubi XXVII — David Smith, 1965, Bolton Landing, New York, USA Burnished stainless steel cubes and cylinders balanced in a precarious vertical arrangement. The surfaces catch and scatter light through hand-polished circular patterns. One of the last works in Smith’s Cubi series, completed shortly before his death. Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum, New York.

Hudson River Landscape — David Smith, 1951, Bolton Landing, New York, USA Welded steel “drawing in space” — linear steel elements describing a landscape without enclosing mass. Demonstrated that sculpture could operate like a line drawing, defining form through edges rather than volume. Whitney Museum of American Art, New York.

Maman — Louise Bourgeois, 1999, New York, USA (exhibited worldwide) A 30-foot-tall bronze, stainless steel, and marble spider carrying a sac of marble eggs. Bourgeois associated the spider with her mother — a weaver and protector. The scale shifts the spider from a creature of fear to one of sheltering architecture. Multiple casts installed at the Guggenheim Bilbao, Tate Modern, National Gallery of Canada, and elsewhere.

Cell (Eyes and Mirrors) — Louise Bourgeois, 1989–1993, New York, USA Enclosed room-sized installation with mirrors and marble eye forms. Part of Bourgeois’s Cells series — architectural enclosures containing psychologically charged objects that explore memory, fear, and the body. Tate Modern, London.

Untitled Stack — Peter Voulkos, c. 1960, Los Angeles, USA Monumental stacked and torn ceramic form. Voulkos gouged, ripped, and reassembled clay with gestural intensity, treating the ceramic vessel as a vehicle for Abstract Expressionist energy. Broke ceramics free from the craft tradition and established it as fine art.

Reclining Figure — Henry Moore, 1951, Festival of Britain, London, England Cast bronze figure with hollowed torso and pierced forms. Moore’s organic abstraction — influenced by both Surrealism and natural erosion — brought modernist sculpture to a broad public audience through postwar public commissions worldwide.

Minimalism (c. 1960–1975)

Sculpture & Objects

Untitled (Stack) — Donald Judd, 1967, New York, USA Ten identical galvanized-iron boxes mounted vertically on a wall with equal spaces between them. Industrially fabricated to Judd’s specifications. No composition, no hierarchy, no focal point — just repetition and interval. Museum of Modern Art, New York.

Untitled (six boxes) — Donald Judd, 1974, Marfa, Texas, USA Six large plywood boxes in a line. Installed permanently at the Chinati Foundation in Marfa, which Judd established to display art in conditions he controlled. The relationship between the objects, the architecture, and the West Texas landscape is the work.

Equivalent VIII — Carl Andre, 1966, New York, USA 120 firebricks arranged in a two-layer rectangular formation on the gallery floor. No adhesive, no alteration, no pedestal. Provoked public outrage when acquired by the Tate Gallery in 1972, becoming one of the most debated Minimalist works. Tate Modern, London.

144 Magnesium Square — Carl Andre, 1969, New York, USA A 12-by-12 grid of thin magnesium plates laid flat on the floor. Visitors are invited to walk across it, making the viewer’s body and movement part of the experience. The sculpture is the surface itself.

monument 4 for those who have been killed in ambush (to P.K. who reminded me about death) — Dan Flavin, 1966, New York, USA Red fluorescent light tubes arranged in a vertical formation. The colored light fills the surrounding space, dissolving the boundary between the object and its environment. Flavin’s work transformed commercial fixtures into immaterial sculpture.

Untitled (to the “innovator” of Wheeling Peachblow) — Dan Flavin, 1968, New York, USA Fluorescent light installation using daylight, pink, and yellow tubes in a corner arrangement. The work changes dramatically depending on ambient light conditions. Dan Flavin Institute, Bridgehampton, New York.

A Wall for Apricots — Anne Truitt, 1968, Washington, D.C., USA Tall painted wooden column in apricot tones. Truitt’s carefully chosen colors and proportions carry emotional resonance within a Minimalist vocabulary. Her work proves that geometric simplicity and feeling are not mutually exclusive. National Gallery of Art, Washington, D.C.

Design Legacy Objects

Judd Furniture — Donald Judd, 1970s–1990s, Marfa, Texas, USA Judd designed chairs, desks, beds, and shelving using the same principles as his sculpture: simple geometry, honest materials (pine, plywood, copper, aluminum), no ornament. Now produced by the Judd Foundation, these objects demonstrate that Minimalist sculpture and functional design share the same logic.

Post-Minimalism & Process Art (c. 1966–1980s)

Sculpture & Objects

Hang Up — Eva Hesse, 1966, New York, USA A wrapped and painted frame with a steel wire looping outward from the top corner, arcing across the floor, and returning to the bottom corner. The wire’s sag and spring are left to gravity. Art Institute of Chicago.

Repetition Nineteen III — Eva Hesse, 1968, New York, USA Nineteen translucent fiberglass cylinders, each slightly different due to the handmade process. Arranged on the floor in no fixed pattern — the configuration changes with each installation. Museum of Modern Art, New York.

Untitled (Felt Piece) — Robert Morris, 1967–1968, New York, USA Industrial felt cut into strips and hung from a wall peg. Gravity determines the form. Each installation produces a different configuration. The same material, the same cuts, a different sculpture every time.

Splashing — Richard Serra, 1968, New York, USA Molten lead thrown against the junction of wall and floor at the Leo Castelli Warehouse gallery. The lead cooled and hardened in place. When the show ended, the work was destroyed. Existed only as documentation — raising questions about permanence, site, and what constitutes a sculpture.

Tilted Arc — Richard Serra, 1981, New York, USA A 120-foot-long, 12-foot-high curved wall of Cor-ten steel installed in Federal Plaza, Manhattan. Designed to alter pedestrians’ experience of the space. Controversial from installation; destroyed by government order in 1989 after public hearings. The most famous case study in public art, site-specificity, and the conflict between artistic vision and public use.

Fallen — Lynda Benglis, 1968, New York, USA Pigmented latex poured directly onto the gallery floor, allowed to flow and pool according to gravity and the material’s viscosity. The hardened form records the event of its making. Simultaneously painting and sculpture, controlled and accidental.

1,000 Pounds of Clay — Barry Le Va, 1968–1969, New York, USA Unfired clay dropped and scattered across a gallery floor. Le Va called his works “distributions.” The arrangement results from the act of throwing, not deliberate composition.

Installation Art (c. 1970s–Present)

Immersive Environments

Infinity Mirrored Room — The Souls of Millions of Light Years Away — Yayoi Kusama, 2013, Japan (exhibited worldwide) A mirrored room with LED lights suspended at varying heights, creating the illusion of infinite space. The viewer’s body becomes part of the visual field, multiplied endlessly. Part of Kusama’s ongoing Infinity Rooms series, begun in the 1960s. The Broad, Los Angeles.

The Weather Project — Olafur Eliasson, 2003, London, England A giant artificial sun made from mono-frequency lamps and a mirrored ceiling installed in the Turbine Hall at Tate Modern. Visitors lay on the floor and watched their reflections, creating a collective social experience. Attended by over two million people. Demonstrated that art could function as shared atmospheric experience.

Your Rainbow Panorama — Olafur Eliasson, 2011, Aarhus, Denmark A 150-meter circular walkway of colored glass installed on the roof of the ARoS Aarhus Art Museum. Visitors walk through a continuous spectrum of color while viewing the city below. Architecture, sculpture, and optical experience merged.

A Subtlety, or the Marvelous Sugar Baby — Kara Walker, 2014, Brooklyn, New York, USA A 75-foot-long, 35-foot-tall sphinx-like figure coated in refined white sugar, installed inside the soon-to-be-demolished Domino Sugar Refinery. Surrounded by smaller figures made of sugar and molasses. The work used architecture, material, scale, and the site’s industrial history to confront the relationship between sugar production, slavery, and the exploitation of Black labor.

Sunflower Seeds (Kui Hua Zi) — Ai Weiwei, 2010, London, England One hundred million handmade porcelain sunflower seeds, individually crafted and hand-painted by 1,600 artisans in Jingdezhen, China, spread across the Turbine Hall floor at Tate Modern. Visitors initially walked on them. The work addresses mass production, individuality, Chinese labor, and collective identity through sheer accumulation.

Forever Bicycles — Ai Weiwei, various versions 2011–present Installations of hundreds to thousands of interconnected bicycle frames, stacked and interlocking without wheels. Uses the Chinese “Forever” brand bicycle — once ubiquitous, now largely replaced by cars. Addresses mass production, cultural change, and the transformation of a utilitarian object into monumental form. Exhibited globally.

Light & Space

Roden Crater — James Turrell, begun 1977, Flagstaff, Arizona, USA An extinct volcanic cinder cone being transformed into a monumental naked-eye observatory with tunnels, chambers, and apertures designed to frame celestial events. Decades in construction, it represents the most ambitious integration of art, architecture, and natural light ever attempted.

Double Negative — Michael Heizer, 1969–1970, Overton, Nevada, USA Two enormous trenches cut into the edge of a desert mesa, displacing 244,000 tons of rock. The sculpture is the void — the removed material, not the remaining form. One of the defining works of Land Art, it expanded sculpture to a geological scale. Museum of Contemporary Art, Los Angeles (owns the site).

Spiral Jetty — Robert Smithson, 1970, Rozel Point, Great Salt Lake, Utah, USA A 1,500-foot-long coil of black basalt, earth, and salt crystals extending into the Great Salt Lake. Periodically submerged and revealed as lake levels change. The work’s relationship to time, entropy, and natural processes — its surface encrusted with salt crystals, its visibility dependent on drought — is central to its meaning. Dia Art Foundation.
