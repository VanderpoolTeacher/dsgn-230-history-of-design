# Early Modernism & the Avant-Garde — Key Examples

Visual reference guide for DSGN 230 — Survey of Design History.

---

## Cubist Sculpture & Design (c. 1910–1920)

### Sculpture & Objects

**Guitar** — Pablo Picasso, 1912–1914, Paris, France
Constructed from sheet metal and wire. One of the first sculptures made by assembling flat materials rather than carving or modeling. The sound hole projects outward as a cylinder, reversing the expected concavity. Museum of Modern Art, New York.

**Head of a Woman (Fernande)** — Pablo Picasso, 1909, Paris, France
Bronze head with fractured, faceted planes replacing smooth contours. One of the earliest three-dimensional translations of Cubist painting principles. Metropolitan Museum of Art and Centre Pompidou.

**Glass of Absinthe** — Pablo Picasso, 1914, Paris, France
Painted bronze sculpture topped with a real absinthe spoon and cast sugar cube. Six casts exist, each painted differently — identical forms carrying different identities through surface treatment. Museum of Modern Art, New York.

**Woman Walking** — Alexander Archipenko, 1912, Paris, France
Figure composed of concavities and voids where classical sculpture would place convex masses. Archipenko treated empty space as a positive sculptural element.

**Médrano II** — Alexander Archipenko, 1913 (reconstructed 1957), Paris, France
Polychromed assemblage of wood, metal, and glass depicting a circus figure. Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum, New York.

**Man with a Guitar** — Jacques Lipchitz, 1915, Paris, France
Interlocking stone planes compressed into a vertical figure. Museum of Modern Art, New York.

### Graphic Design & Typography

**Cubist Collages** — Pablo Picasso and Georges Braque, 1912–1914, Paris, France
Picasso's *Still Life with Chair Caning* (1912) and Braque's papier collé works introduced foreign materials into pictorial compositions. Direct ancestors of photomontage and mixed-media graphic design.

**Futurist Typography — *Zang Tumb Tumb*** — F. T. Marinetti, 1914, Milan, Italy
"Words-in-freedom" typography exploding text across pages in varying sizes, weights, and orientations. Profoundly influenced twentieth-century experimental typography.

## Constructivism (c. 1913–1930s)

### Sculpture & Architecture

**Monument to the Third International (Model)** — Vladimir Tatlin, 1919–1920, Petrograd, Russia
Spiraling double-helix tower of iron and glass, planned at 400 meters tall. Interior volumes (cube, pyramid, cylinder) would rotate at different speeds. Never built, but its model became the icon of Constructivist ambition.

**Corner Counter-Relief** — Vladimir Tatlin, 1914–1915, Moscow, Russia
Metal, wire, and wood assemblage suspended across a room corner by cables. Eliminated the pedestal, wall, and floor as conventional supports.

**Linear Construction in Space No. 1** — Naum Gabo, 1942–1943 (concept from 1920s), Russia / England
Transparent Perspex sheets strung with nylon thread. Tate Modern, London.

**Kinetic Construction (Standing Wave)** — Naum Gabo, 1919–1920, Moscow, Russia
A single metal rod vibrated by a motor to create the illusion of a standing wave — one of the first kinetic sculptures. Tate Modern, London.

### Graphic Design & Typography

***Beat the Whites with the Red Wedge*** — El Lissitzky, 1919, Russia
Propaganda poster using pure geometric forms — a red triangle piercing a white circle. Foundational to modern poster design.

**Proun Compositions** — El Lissitzky, 1919–1927, Russia / Germany
Spatial compositions as paintings, prints, and room-sized environments. The *Proun Room* (1923) dissolved the distinction between artwork and architecture.

***Books (Please)! In All Branches of Knowledge*** — Alexander Rodchenko, 1924, Moscow, Russia
Photomontage poster for Lengiz featuring Lilya Brik. One of the most iconic images in graphic design history.

**Workers' Club** — Alexander Rodchenko, 1925, Paris, France (exhibited)
Complete interior design for a workers' reading room at the 1925 Paris Exposition. Demonstrated Constructivist principles in everyday environments.

## Dada & the Readymade (c. 1916–1924)

### Objects & Sculpture

**Fountain** — Marcel Duchamp (as "R. Mutt"), 1917, New York, USA
Porcelain urinal signed and submitted to an open exhibition. The most discussed artwork of the twentieth century. Authorized replicas in major museums worldwide.

**Bicycle Wheel** — Marcel Duchamp, 1913, Paris, France
Bicycle wheel mounted upside-down on a kitchen stool. 1951 replica in the Museum of Modern Art, New York.

**Bottle Rack (Porte-bouteilles)** — Marcel Duchamp, 1914, Paris, France
Galvanized-iron bottle-drying rack purchased from a department store. No alteration. The readymade at its purest.

**Gift (Cadeau)** — Man Ray, 1921, Paris, France
Flatiron with fourteen tacks glued to its smooth surface. Transforms a domestic tool into something dangerous.

**Indestructible Object** — Man Ray, 1923 (reconstructed 1964), Paris, France
Metronome with a photograph of an eye clipped to its arm. Museum of Modern Art, New York.

### Environments

**Merzbau** — Kurt Schwitters, begun 1923, Hanover, Germany
Room-filling sculptural environment built from found materials over more than a decade. Destroyed in Allied bombing in 1943. Anticipated installation art and art as ongoing process.

### Graphic Design & Photomontage

***Cut with the Kitchen Knife Dada Through the Last Weimar Beer-Belly Cultural Epoch of Germany*** — Hannah Höch, 1919–1920, Berlin, Germany
Large-scale photomontage combining press photographs into a politically charged composition. Nationalgalerie, Berlin.

***Merz* Magazine** — Kurt Schwitters, 1923–1932, Hanover, Germany
Experimental typography, collage layouts, and contributions from Constructivists, Dadaists, and De Stijl artists.

## Surrealist Objects (c. 1924–1940s)

### Objects & Sculpture

**Object (Le Déjeuner en fourrure)** — Meret Oppenheim, 1936, Paris, France
Teacup, saucer, and spoon lined with gazelle fur. Museum of Modern Art, New York.

**Suspended Ball (Boule suspendue)** — Alberto Giacometti, 1930–1931, Paris, France
Cleft sphere hanging above a crescent in an open cage. Fondation Giacometti, Paris.

**The Palace at 4 a.m.** — Alberto Giacometti, 1932, Paris, France
Open wooden framework enclosing fragile figures. Museum of Modern Art, New York.

**Lobster Telephone** — Salvador Dalí, 1936, Paris, France
Functioning telephone with plaster lobster replacing the handset. Examples at the Tate, Walker Art Center, and Dalí Theatre-Museum.

**Mae West Lips Sofa** — Salvador Dalí (with Edward James), 1937, Paris / England
Pink felt sofa shaped as Mae West's lips. Anticipated Pop Art by two decades. Dalí Theatre-Museum, Figueres.

### Design Influence

**Surrealist Window Displays** — Salvador Dalí, 1939, New York, USA
Window displays for Bonwit Teller department store. Demonstrated Surrealism's growing influence on retail design.

**Schiaparelli × Dalí Collaborations** — 1936–1937, Paris, France
The Lobster Dress, the Shoe Hat, and the Skeleton Dress. One of the earliest fusions of avant-garde art and high fashion.

## Abstract Expressionist Sculpture (c. 1940s–1960s)

**Cubi XXVII** — David Smith, 1965, Bolton Landing, New York, USA
Burnished stainless steel cubes and cylinders in precarious vertical arrangement. Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum, New York.

**Hudson River Landscape** — David Smith, 1951, Bolton Landing, New York, USA
Welded steel "drawing in space." Whitney Museum of American Art, New York.

**Maman** — Louise Bourgeois, 1999, exhibited worldwide
30-foot-tall bronze, stainless steel, and marble spider. Multiple casts at the Guggenheim Bilbao, Tate Modern, National Gallery of Canada.

**Cell (Eyes and Mirrors)** — Louise Bourgeois, 1989–1993, New York, USA
Enclosed room-sized installation with mirrors and marble eye forms. Tate Modern, London.

**Untitled Stack** — Peter Voulkos, c. 1960, Los Angeles, USA
Monumental stacked and torn ceramic form. Broke ceramics free from craft tradition.

**Reclining Figure** — Henry Moore, 1951, London, England
Cast bronze with hollowed torso and pierced forms. Brought modernist sculpture to a broad public audience.

## Minimalism (c. 1960–1975)

**Untitled (Stack)** — Donald Judd, 1967, New York, USA
Ten identical galvanized-iron boxes mounted vertically on a wall. Museum of Modern Art, New York.

**Equivalent VIII** — Carl Andre, 1966, New York, USA
120 firebricks in a rectangular floor formation. Tate Modern, London.

**144 Magnesium Square** — Carl Andre, 1969, New York, USA
12-by-12 grid of thin metal plates on the floor. Visitors walk across it.

***monument* 4 for those who have been killed in ambush** — Dan Flavin, 1966, New York, USA
Red fluorescent light tubes in vertical formation. Light fills the surrounding space.

**A Wall for Apricots** — Anne Truitt, 1968, Washington, D.C., USA
Tall painted wooden column in apricot tones. National Gallery of Art, Washington, D.C.

**Judd Furniture** — Donald Judd, 1970s–1990s, Marfa, Texas, USA
Chairs, desks, beds, and shelving using the same principles as his sculpture. Now produced by the Judd Foundation.

## Post-Minimalism & Process Art (c. 1966–1980s)

**Hang Up** — Eva Hesse, 1966, New York, USA
Wrapped frame with a steel wire looping outward, arcing across the floor. Art Institute of Chicago.

**Repetition Nineteen III** — Eva Hesse, 1968, New York, USA
Nineteen translucent fiberglass cylinders, each slightly different. Museum of Modern Art, New York.

**Untitled (Felt Piece)** — Robert Morris, 1967–1968, New York, USA
Industrial felt cut into strips and hung from a wall peg. Gravity determines the form.

**Splashing** — Richard Serra, 1968, New York, USA
Molten lead thrown against the wall-floor junction. Destroyed when the show ended.

**Tilted Arc** — Richard Serra, 1981, New York, USA
120-foot curved steel wall in Federal Plaza. Destroyed by government order in 1989. The most famous case study in public art and site-specificity.

**Fallen** — Lynda Benglis, 1968, New York, USA
Pigmented latex poured directly onto the gallery floor. Simultaneously painting and sculpture.

## Installation Art (c. 1970s–Present)

**Infinity Mirrored Room — The Souls of Millions of Light Years Away** — Yayoi Kusama, 2013, exhibited worldwide
Mirrored room with LED lights creating infinite space. The Broad, Los Angeles.

**The Weather Project** — Olafur Eliasson, 2003, London, England
Artificial sun in the Turbine Hall at Tate Modern. Over two million visitors.

**Your Rainbow Panorama** — Olafur Eliasson, 2011, Aarhus, Denmark
150-meter circular walkway of colored glass on the ARoS Aarhus Art Museum roof.

**A Subtlety** — Kara Walker, 2014, Brooklyn, New York, USA
75-foot sphinx-like figure coated in white sugar inside the Domino Sugar Refinery.

**Sunflower Seeds** — Ai Weiwei, 2010, London, England
100 million handmade porcelain sunflower seeds at Tate Modern.

**Forever Bicycles** — Ai Weiwei, 2011–present, exhibited globally
Hundreds to thousands of interconnected bicycle frames without wheels.

**Roden Crater** — James Turrell, begun 1977, Flagstaff, Arizona, USA
Extinct volcanic cinder cone transformed into a naked-eye observatory. Decades in construction.

**Double Negative** — Michael Heizer, 1969–1970, Nevada, USA
Two enormous trenches cut into a desert mesa, displacing 244,000 tons of rock. Museum of Contemporary Art, Los Angeles.

**Spiral Jetty** — Robert Smithson, 1970, Great Salt Lake, Utah, USA
1,500-foot coil of basalt, earth, and salt crystals in the lake. Dia Art Foundation.
